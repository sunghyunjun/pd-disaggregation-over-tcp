\begin{abstract}
Prefill--decode disaggregation usually relies on RDMA or NVLink, which cheap single-GPU instances lack. For a TCP-linked fleet of such instances, we ask whether disaggregation is worth considering once chunked prefill, which attacks the same decode stalls, is tuned, and if so, where. Serving Gemma~4 26B and 31B with stock vLLM and NIXL/UCX on AWS, we measured both costs: a replica's longest inter-token gap followed the chunk size (26B, 4K inputs: 48~ms at 2,560 tokens, 21~ms at 512), and disaggregation's added time to first token (TTFT) was mostly KV transfer, which a larger UCX segment made 3.4$\times$ faster at low load. At 4K inputs and in chat, against tuned chunks, disaggregation served more per GPU under none of the latency targets our measured rates could rank; it won, by about 3$\times$ for 26B, only against replicas held at 2,560 tokens, near vLLM v0.30.0's floor for Gemma~4 with video enabled. A step-time model calibrated on our runs predicts that at low load and a 50~ms gap limit, inputs of roughly 14K--37K tokens (lower edge 11K--34K within its uncertainty) need so many chunks that disaggregation over TCP meets TTFT limits of a second or more that no chunk meets, given a small KV cache. Tune the chunk first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Serving a request to a large language model takes two phases: \emph{prefill}, which processes the whole input at once, and \emph{decode}, which produces output tokens one at a time. When both share a GPU, a prefill scheduled into the same step as ongoing decodes lengthens that step, and every streaming request on that GPU pauses. Two remedies exist. \emph{Chunked prefill} splits a long prefill into pieces that are batched with decodes, which bounds the work any one step carries~\cite{sarathi2023,sarathi}. \emph{Prefill--decode (PD) disaggregation} runs the two phases on different GPUs and ships the KV cache from the prefill GPU to the decode GPU~\cite{distserve,splitwise,mooncake}.

Disaggregation pays for its isolation with a KV-cache transfer, and published systems run that transfer over fast links: InfiniBand~\cite{splitwise}, RDMA~\cite{mooncake}, or NVLink inside one node~\cite{distserve}. The cheapest single-GPU cloud instances offer none of these. On AWS, the 2xlarge sizes of the g6e, g7, and g7e families have one GPU, no Elastic Fabric Adapter (EFA), and no ENA Express~\cite{awsspecs}; the 8xlarge sizes of the same families add EFA at 1.6--2$\times$ the hourly price~\cite{awsefa,awspricing}. Public documentation discourages disaggregation over TCP: llm-d calls it ``extremely slow'' and ``targeted for local development''~\cite{llmddocs}, and NVIDIA Dynamo warns of a 200--500$\times$ slower TTFT without RDMA and recommends aggregated serving instead~\cite{dynamodocs}. Few public measurements show how slow the TCP path is, or whether disaggregation over it still beats a well-configured colocated deployment.

Our question comes from scaling out on such instances. A fleet of single-GPU nodes linked by TCP has several levers against prefill interference and repeated prefill work: chunked prefill, disaggregation, prefix-cache-aware routing as in llm-d~\cite{llmdarch}, and KV-cache offloading as in LMCache~\cite{lmcache}. Chunked prefill and disaggregation attack the same problem, the decode stalls that other requests' prefills cause, and only disaggregation pays for its fix with a network transfer. We therefore ask whether disaggregation over TCP is worth considering at all once the chunk size is tuned, and if so, where.

We measured both remedies on the same hardware. We served two Gemma~4 models with stock vLLM on one to three single-GPU instances, disaggregated them with vLLM's NIXL connector over UCX's TCP transport, and judged each request against a service-level objective (SLO) on its TTFT and on the longest gap between its output tokens. Our first comparison used replicas at a 2,560-token chunk, the smallest our stack accepted with images disabled. Two rounds of AI-assisted mock peer review found that disabling every modality allows smaller chunks and that the replicas' request proxy capped concurrent connections at 100; we added two measurement rounds that swept the chunk size and removed the cap, and we committed our predictions for the last round before running it. Our tuned-chunk measurements stop at 4K-token inputs, so for longer inputs we calibrate a step-time model on the measured runs and use it to locate the boundary.

We ask three research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} How large are the two costs on cloud hardware: the stall that a prefill chunk imposes on colocated decodes, and the TTFT that a KV transfer over TCP adds?
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} At the input lengths we measured (4K tokens and chat), does a tuned chunk leave any SLO in which disaggregation over TCP serves more per GPU?
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} Where does chunked prefill run out, and what decides whether disaggregation over TCP can take over there?
\end{itemize}
In short: a chunk of 512--1,024 tokens kept the 26B replicas' median longest gap at 21--28~ms, and disaggregation's added TTFT was 61--85\% KV transfer at low load (RQ1). At 4K inputs and in chat, no SLO that our measured rates could rank favored disaggregation; it won only when the chunk could not shrink (RQ2). Our model predicts that at low load chunking runs out for long inputs, from roughly 14K tokens at a 50~ms gap limit for 26B FP8 (11K--34K within its uncertainty), where disaggregation over TCP can meet TTFT limits of a second or more that no chunk meets, given a small KV cache (RQ3).

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item measured sizes of both costs on rented single-GPU nodes, including the TCP transfer path: the UCX segment size, NIXL's splitting of a transfer over up to four connections, the cloud's per-flow cap, and a KV-volume and TTFT model (Section~\ref{sec:costs});
  \item a head-to-head at equal GPU count with tuned chunks, a map over 192 SLOs, and the exception when the chunk cannot shrink (Section~\ref{sec:h2hsec});
  \item a step-time model, calibrated on measured runs and checked against measurements it was not fitted to, that estimates, with its uncertainty, where chunked prefill runs out and shows how input length, gap limit, KV size, and link speed move the crossover (Section~\ref{sec:boundary});
  \item a procedure that places disaggregation among chunk tuning, prefix-aware routing, and KV offloading (Section~\ref{sec:procedure-fleet}), the measurement pitfalls we met (Appendix~\ref{app:pitfalls}), and the harness, per-request data, and four rounds of mock review.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Disaggregated serving.} DistServe splits prefill and decode, provisions each phase for its own latency target, and measures per-GPU goodput at an SLO attainment target~\cite{distserve}; we use the same metric. With 25~Gbps between nodes, DistServe keeps the KV transfer on intra-node NVLink. Splitwise runs the phases on different machine types over InfiniBand and overlaps the transfer with prefill layer by layer~\cite{splitwise}. Mooncake builds a KV-cache-centric architecture over RDMA~\cite{mooncake}. TetriInfer~\cite{tetriinfer}, D\'ej\`aVu~\cite{dejavu}, P/D-Serve~\cite{pdserve}, and KVDirect, which pulls the KV cache from the decode side as vLLM's connector does~\cite{kvdirect}, belong to the same family and assume a fast interconnect.

\paragraph{Colocation and when to split.} SARATHI introduced chunked prefill~\cite{sarathi2023}, and Sarathi-Serve built stall-free batching on it, trading prefill throughput against decode stalls through the chunk size~\cite{sarathi}. semi-PD disaggregates compute inside one GPU while sharing KV storage~\cite{semipd}. TaiChi unifies the two with prefill-heavy and decode-heavy instances whose ratio and chunk sizes are set by configurable sliders, and reports that aggregation suits tight TTFT and relaxed time per output token (TPOT) while disaggregation suits strict TPOT and relaxed TTFT~\cite{taichi}. vLLM's documentation states that disaggregated prefill does not improve throughput, motivates it by tail inter-token latency, and notes that chunked prefill with a proper chunk size can reach the same goal but that the right size is hard to find~\cite{vllmdocs}. We test that trade-off with stock vLLM over cloud TCP. Our conclusion leans against disaggregation more than TaiChi's; candidate reasons are the metric (each request's longest gap rather than its mean per-token time), the interconnect, and the models and scale, which we did not separate. EcoServe targets clusters with commodity interconnects, where full disaggregation depends on fast links, and proposes partial disaggregation~\cite{ecoserve}; ThunderServe and HexGen-2 schedule phase-split serving across heterogeneous cloud GPUs and networks~\cite{thunderserve,hexgen2}.

\paragraph{KV transfer layer.} vLLM~\cite{vllm} moves the KV cache with NIXL~\cite{nixl}, which wraps transports including UCX~\cite{ucx}; without RDMA, UCX uses TCP. CacheGen compresses the KV cache to reduce network traffic~\cite{cachegen}. Prefill-as-a-Service sends the small KV caches of hybrid-attention models between datacenters over commodity Ethernet~\cite{prfaas}, and PDD disaggregates across clusters linked by wide-area TCP~\cite{pdd}. The llm-d project measured GPU-to-GPU transfers over 100~Gbps TCP at 0.55~GB/s with UCX and 4.7--4.9~GB/s with UCCL~\cite{llmdblog}. Mitra et al.\ find disaggregation most effective for prefill-heavy traffic and larger models~\cite{beyondbuzz}.

\paragraph{Reusing and placing KV caches.} Prefix-cache-aware routing sends a request to the replica that already holds its prefix, so the prefill shrinks without moving any KV cache; llm-d's scheduler scores replicas by KV-cache affinity~\cite{llmdarch}. LMCache stores KV caches outside the engine and shares them across engines and queries~\cite{lmcache}; loading a KV cache from another node is a transfer of at least the same size as a disaggregated one. We did not measure either; Section~\ref{sec:procedure-fleet} uses our transfer measurements to reason about them.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Etalon argues that TTFT, time between tokens, and their percentiles miss what users experience and proposes a fluidity index~\cite{etalon}; Andes defines a quality of experience over each request's token delivery timeline~\cite{andes}. We use a simpler per-request criterion, the longest gap between consecutive output tokens.

\paragraph{Where this work sits.} We do not design a system. We measure stock vLLM on rented single-GPU nodes, compare disaggregation with colocated replicas whose chunk size we tune, explain the transfer path, and calibrate a simple model to say where the comparison turns. We did not find a measurement of this setting, but our search was not exhaustive.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Environment}
We used three single-GPU instance types in one availability zone of AWS us-east-1 (Table~\ref{tab:instances}). None of these sizes supports EFA or ENA Express~\cite{awsspecs}, so all traffic between nodes is TCP. Within the zone, one TCP flow reached at most 4.97~Gbps by iperf, matching the documented 5~Gbps single-flow limit~\cite{awsbw}. Inside a cluster placement group, an AWS feature that places instances close together, one flow reached 9.54~Gbps (documented: 10~Gbps), and eight parallel flows reached 49.72~Gbps, the g7e's nominal 50~Gbps. Load generation and request routing ran on a separate CPU instance (c6i.xlarge in early rounds, c6i.2xlarge in the last three; Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

We used vLLM v0.30.0 (NIXL 1.4.1) with NIXL's pull-based connector, in which the decode node reads the KV cache from the prefill node. Unless stated otherwise, disaggregated runs used seven UCX environment variables that we tuned in early rounds (Appendix~\ref{app:ucx}), except the three runs with an 8,192-token prefill chunk, which set only the two segment sizes. Servers used a GPU memory utilization of 0.90 (except 0.42 in the KV-matched single-node runs and 0.70 in the host-staging runs), at most 256 concurrent sequences, and a 32,768-token context (16,384 for the 31B FP8 runs of the cross-GPU comparison). The disaggregated side kept its defaults: a 2,560-token chunk on both nodes (the three runs above used 8,192 on the prefill node), 256 sequences on the decode node, and NIXL's default four worker threads.

\subsection{Models and KV size}\label{sec:kv}
We served two Gemma~4 models~\cite{gemma4}. 26B-A4B is a mixture-of-experts model that activates 3.8B of its 25.2B parameters per token; 31B is dense (30.7B parameters). Both interleave sliding-window and global attention. We mainly used NVIDIA's NVFP4 checkpoints, whose KV cache is FP8~\cite{nvfp4ckpt}, and for comparison FP8 (weights and activations, with dynamic activation scales) and BF16 checkpoints of 26B, whose KV cache is BF16. Because sliding-window layers keep only the most recent tokens, the transferred KV volume has a global part that grows with the input length $L$ and a local part that stops growing at the window $W$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vol}
V(L) = b_{\mathrm{g}}\,L + b_{\mathrm{w}} \min(L, W).
\end{equation}
For 26B NVFP4, vLLM reported 140, 260, and 412.5~MiB per request at 4K, 16K, and 32K inputs, which gives $b_{\mathrm{g}} \approx 10$~KiB per token and a saturated local part of about 100~MiB, consistent with $W = 1{,}024$ and $b_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 100$~KiB per token; chat requests (median input about 100 tokens, mean about 230) moved 25--26~MiB on average, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:vol} predicts from their input lengths. The FP8 and BF16 checkpoints transfer twice as much (280~MiB at 4K); 31B NVFP4 transfers 560~MiB at 4K. vLLM reports these volumes as ``MB''; they are mebibytes, and we convert rates as $\text{Gbps} = 0.008389 \times \text{MiB}/\text{s}$.

\subsection{Configurations}\label{sec:config}
We compared two approaches at equal GPU count. In the \emph{replica} configuration, each node runs a colocated server that does both prefill and decode, and a round-robin proxy spreads requests over the nodes. Replicas use chunked prefill; the \emph{chunk} is vLLM's maximum number of tokens per scheduler step. In the \emph{disaggregated} configuration there are prefill-only (P) and decode-only (D) nodes, written $x$P$y$D for $x$ prefill and $y$ decode nodes.

Three practical details shaped the comparison. First, for Gemma~4, vLLM rejected chunks smaller than 2,496 tokens, the budget of one video item, with images disabled or with images and audio disabled, and accepted them with images, audio, and video all disabled (every modality set to 0 in \texttt{-{}-limit-mm-per-prompt}); we did not test other combinations. Our early rounds therefore used 2,560 tokens; the last two swept 256--2,048 and added 512 for 26B. With every modality disabled, a 2,560-token chunk failed at engine start, so the 2,560 controls kept images disabled only. Second, until the last round the replicas' proxy, a small aiohttp program, allowed at most 100 concurrent connections, and requests beyond that waited in the proxy; the eleven replica runs that reached the cap are caution data (Appendix~\ref{sec:proxy}). Third, early disaggregated runs used vLLM's test proxy for NIXL (FastAPI with httpx); the last two rounds added an aiohttp proxy with the same request flow and no connection limit (Appendix~\ref{sec:proxy}).

\subsection{Workloads}
\emph{Chat} uses ShareGPT conversations~\cite{sharegpt} (median input 76--141 and median output 24--177 tokens, depending on the sampled subset); prompts went to the completions endpoint without a chat template, and with end-of-sequence ignored each output had the length of the reference answer. \emph{Long input} uses synthetic requests with 4,096, 16,384, or 32,000 input tokens and 512 output tokens. \emph{Trace} replays 600 requests of the conversation trace released with Splitwise (median input 996, output 217 tokens)~\cite{azuretrace}. Requests arrive as a Poisson process. A measurement point at a total rate of $r$ requests/s sends $\max(100, \lceil 180r\rceil)$ requests, about 180~s at 0.56 requests/s or more. The same rate and repeat index use the same seed in every configuration, so comparisons at a rate are paired. We report rates per GPU unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Metrics and SLO}\label{sec:slo}
We judge each request individually. Under the \emph{strict SLO}, a request must have TTFT (time from sending the request to the first output token) of at most 1~s and a longest inter-token gap of at most 50~ms; the \emph{relaxed SLO} uses 3~s and 250~ms. We use the longest gap because one long pause in a 500-token answer is 0.2\% of its gaps and vanishes from percentile statistics, yet it is the pause a user sees. A request with a single output token has no gap and is judged on TTFT only, a rule we adopted after the chunk-sweep round; it turned two borderline 31B 1P1D runs at 4 requests/s per GPU from failing (0.899 and 0.878) to passing (0.924 and 0.903) and raised other chat runs by at most 0.055.

\emph{Attainment} is the fraction of requests that meet the SLO. \emph{Supported load} is the highest measured rate at which every repeat reaches 0.9 attainment. \emph{SLO goodput} is the output-token throughput of SLO-meeting requests (the minimum over repeats at that rate), and \emph{cost} is the GPU hourly price divided by SLO goodput, in dollars per million tokens.

The thresholds are design choices. TTFT targets in prior work range from 0.125~s to 30~s~\cite{distserve,mooncake}; 1~s is the classic limit for keeping a user's flow of thought~\cite{nielsen}. For the gap, our pre-registered protocol (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}) used five times each model's decode step, Sarathi-Serve's strict rule~\cite{sarathi}. We switched to a common absolute limit because a relative rule makes faster models stricter in absolute terms (38~ms for 26B BF16 on g7e) and makes models hard to compare; 50~ms is five times the 26B decode step on the slowest GPU we used. Section~\ref{sec:who} shows how each verdict changes over a grid of TTFT and gap limits.

\subsection{Procedure, data selection, and statistics}\label{sec:procedure}
All reported runs had the prefix cache disabled. Some early runs had it enabled while replaying the same seeded requests, so later points hit cache entries left by earlier ones; we excluded those 122 runs, 5 server-start failures, and 1 discarded run, 128 of 455 runs. Fourteen runs are caution data and not used for verdicts: 5 in which the client-side proxy reached its CPU limit (two of them replica runs that also reached the connection cap) and 9 replica runs that reached the proxy's connection cap. The remaining 313 runs are the \emph{audited} runs. The last three rounds warmed every server with 30 requests; earlier rounds did not, and dropping each run's first five requests raised attainment by at most 0.019, lowered it by at most 0.029, and flipped no verdict.

Median TTFT varied by up to 10\% across repeats. Points well above 0.9 attainment varied by at most 0.03 within a round; points near 0.9 varied by up to $\pm$0.10 and could flip a verdict, and we call a point \emph{borderline} if any repeat falls between 0.85 and 0.95; low points varied more (0.37--0.64). For decisive points we report 95\% intervals from a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples repeats and then 20 time blocks within each run (Appendix~\ref{app:ci}). Disaggregated runs with vLLM's test proxy, whose garbage collection paused streams (Appendix~\ref{sec:proxy}), remain in the data; where both proxies were measured, verdicts use the better of the two. Disaggregated serving gave the same outputs as a single node, within the nondeterminism that 26B showed even on one node (Appendix~\ref{app:other}). We wrote the predictions for the last round into our lab notebook and committed them before running it (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}). Appendix~\ref{app:data} maps each round to its data folder. Section~\ref{sec:boundary} fits a model to existing runs only and adds no measurements.

\section{Measured costs}\label{sec:costs}

\subsection{System percentiles hide per-request stalls}\label{sec:stalls}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{fig01_request_vs_system.pdf}
\caption{System-wide inter-token latency p99 (log scale) against per-request strict-SLO attainment for the 215 runs (212 audited, 3 caution) with TTFT p99 below 1~s. The shaded region holds runs whose system p99 is within 50~ms yet whose attainment is below 0.9: 21 replica or single-node runs and 7 disaggregated runs (2 of them caution).}
\label{fig:stalls}
\end{figure}

The system-wide p99 of inter-token latency pools every gap of every request, and it can look healthy while many requests still stall (Figure~\ref{fig:stalls}). Of the 215 runs with TTFT p99 below 1~s, 28 had system p99 at or below 50~ms but attainment below 0.9. For example, two g7e replicas serving 26B FP8 with 4K inputs at 0.25 requests/s per GPU had a system p99 of 8.6~ms, yet 48\% of requests had at least one gap over 50~ms. All later verdicts are per request.

\subsection{The chunk size sets the replicas' stall}\label{sec:chunk}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{v1_chunk_stall.pdf}
\caption{Per-request longest gap of colocated replicas by chunk size (two g7e nodes in a placement group). Dot: median over requests; bar: 10th--90th percentile; light dots have a median above 50~ms; dashed and dotted lines mark 50 and 30~ms. (a) 26B NVFP4, 4K input, 1 request/s per GPU. (b) 26B FP8, 4K input, 0.5 requests/s per GPU. (c) 31B NVFP4, ShareGPT chat, 1 request/s per GPU. The 2,560-token points pool repeats from three rounds in (a) and (c); in (b) it is one run. The 512-token points in (a) and (b) come from the last round.}
\label{fig:chunk}
\end{figure}

A replica stalls when another request's prefill joins the step, and the stall lasts as long as that step. With 4K inputs and 512 outputs almost every request overlaps another request's prefill, so its longest gap is roughly the time of a step that carries a full chunk. For 26B NVFP4 that time fell with the chunk (Figure~\ref{fig:chunk}a): the median longest gap was 48.4~ms at 2,560 tokens, 38.8~ms at 1,536, 27.6~ms at 1,024, and 21.4~ms at 512, while median TTFT rose from 132 to 205~ms. Between 1,024 and 2,048 tokens the step grew by about 24~\textmu s per token; below 1,024 it shrank more slowly toward the decode-only step, about 9~ms at this load. The 2,560-token median sat 1.6~ms under the 50~ms limit, so the replicas' verdicts at that chunk hinged on a small margin. A 2,048-token chunk gave 52.3~ms, longer than 2,560; we could not tell whether the even split of a 4K input into two 2,048-token pieces or the different multimodal setting of the 2,560 control causes this.

Heavier FP8 weights make each step longer: at 0.5 requests/s per GPU the median longest gap was 62, 43, and 27~ms at 2,560, 1,024, and 512 tokens, with attainment 0.13, 0.99, and 1.00 (Figure~\ref{fig:chunk}b). For 31B chat the longest gap depends on the longest prompt prefilled on the same replica while a request decodes (Figure~\ref{fig:chunk}c). In the five 2,560-token runs at 1 request/s per GPU, 99--100\% of requests kept every gap under 50~ms when that prompt had fewer than 200 tokens, 42\% with 200--400 tokens, 12\% with 400--800, and almost none above 800 (1 of 323). A 512-token chunk therefore did not help (attainment 0.44, the same as 2,560), and a 256-token chunk did (1.00 in two runs, median longest gap 34~ms, TTFT unchanged at 79~ms).

\subsection{The KV transfer over TCP}\label{sec:transfer}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{v1_transfer.pdf}
\caption{(a) Bandwidth between two g7e nodes in a placement group against the number of parallel TCP flows (iperf). (b) KV transfer rate of 26B NVFP4 (140~MiB per 4K request, 0.25 requests/s per GPU) under three UCX settings; dots are 10~s windows reported by vLLM, black lines are medians, and the dashed line is one flow's limit. The seven-variable run is the one with our aiohttp proxy. (c) Share of socket samples (about 0.12~s apart, longer than a 26B transfer of about 80--90~ms) in which 1--4 of the four worker connections moved more than 64~KiB, among samples in which any moved; the shared fifth connection is not counted (26B at 0.25 and 31B at 0.125 requests/s per GPU).}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

Three things set the transfer rate: a software default, how NIXL splits a transfer, and the cloud's caps. With UCX defaults, the 8~KB TCP segment limited the transfer to a median of 4.45~Gbps, less than half of one flow's limit (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}b). Raising only the send and receive segment sizes to 256~KB lifted it to 14.97~Gbps, a 3.4$\times$ gain at low load. The full set of seven UCX variables gave 13.5--14.2~Gbps, 5--10\% lower: it requested 8~MB socket buffers, and in both seven-variable runs we sampled, the receiver's TCP window scale was 2 (7 with the segment sizes alone or the defaults), which caps the advertised window at 256~KiB; we infer that the kernel capped the requested buffers when the connection opened, but we did not record its limits. The sender was limited by the receive window for about 40\% of its busy time. In our first tuning round, on g7 nodes without a placement group, the seven-variable change cut median TTFT at 0.125 requests/s per GPU from 626 to 466~ms and raised attainment at 0.5 from 0.82 to 0.99.

NIXL (version 1.4.1) hands a transfer to up to four worker threads, each with its own UCX endpoint and TCP connection: a 26B 4K transfer goes out in two or three pieces, a 31B 4K transfer in four, and a short one whole on a shared connection (Appendix~\ref{app:ucx}). Socket samples agree (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}c): four connections each carried 22--27\% of the bytes and a fifth, shared one carried the rest; in most samples during 26B transfers two or three connections moved data, and in 64\% of the samples during 31B transfers all four did.

We express the rate relative to the per-flow cap as $m = \text{effective rate} / C_{\mathrm{flow}}$, taking the median over vLLM's 10~s windows (at the loads that decided verdicts, windows with at least five transfers). At low load $m$ was 1.4--1.6 for 26B and 1.8 for 31B. At the loads where our verdicts were decided it fell to 0.92--1.14 for 26B (3--4 requests/s per GPU; 0.68--0.81 in the 8,192-token prefill runs). About a third of 31B chat transfers (three quarters of the bytes) were split, and they ran at about 0.8 (0.79--0.82 at 4 requests/s per GPU, 0.57 at 6). Under load, the time vLLM reports includes waiting for other transfers, so $m$ there describes the queue rather than the link. With the seven-variable set, under which the sender was receive-window-limited even with four connections active, 31B transfers aggregated 19--21~Gbps, about half of what four iperf flows reached (38~Gbps), which points to a shared limit that we did not identify. More connections alone are therefore not a sure lever.

Other transfer settings did not help: staging the KV cache through host memory, push transfer, and a proposed patch that overlaps the transfer with prefill (Appendix~\ref{app:ucx}).

\subsection{The first-token cost of disaggregation}\label{sec:ttft}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{v1_ttft.pdf}

Disaggregation adds TTFT, and most of it is the KV transfer (Figure~\ref{fig:ttft}): 61\% of the added time for 26B NVFP4 at 0.5 requests/s per GPU (84 of 137~ms), 76\% for 31B at 0.125 (283 of 370~ms), and 85\% for 26B FP8 at 0.25. The residual, 28--87~ms, covers the decode node's first step, scheduling, and the proxy hop, and it would remain with RDMA. At low load the relation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ttft}
\mathrm{TTFT}_{\mathrm{PD}} \approx \mathrm{TTFT}_{\mathrm{coloc}} + \frac{V(L)}{m \times C_{\mathrm{flow}}} + \text{residual},
\end{equation}
with $V(L)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:vol}, $C_{\mathrm{flow}}$ the per-flow cap, and $m$ as defined above. We wrote estimates from this relation into our lab notebook before the three-node round: 235~ms for 26B at 0.5 requests/s per GPU (measured 267~ms, 14\% higher) and 810--830~ms for 31B at 0.125 (measured 853~ms); unlike the predictions for the last round, these were not committed separately, so the repository does not record their order. The relation omits queueing: when we limited the sender's aggregate bandwidth to 1~Gbps (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:bwsweep}), it predicted about 1.5~s against a measured median of 2.19~s; an M/D/1 waiting time (transfer 1.17~s, utilization 0.59) adds 0.83~s on average, giving about 2.3~s. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ttft} is a low-load estimate, and $m$ falls with load. It also shows why long inputs fail: for 26B FP8 at 16K inputs, about 520~MiB take roughly 0.3--0.7~s on top of a prefill of about 0.8~s, and we measured 1P1D's TTFT 465--506~ms above the replicas', beyond a 1~s TTFT limit.

\paragraph{Answer to RQ1.} Both costs are of the expected kind; what we add is their size. The chunk sets the stall: for 26B with 4K inputs the median longest gap fell from 48 to 21~ms as the chunk shrank from 2,560 to 512 tokens, at a median TTFT cost of about 30~ms at 1,024 tokens and 70~ms at 512 (1 request/s per GPU); for dense 31B chat the chunk had to be 256 tokens. Disaggregation's added TTFT was 61--85\% KV transfer at low load. A 256~KB UCX segment made the transfer 3.4$\times$ faster, after which the per-flow cap and NIXL's splitting set its rate, and under load transfers queued.

\section{Where chunked prefill runs out}\label{sec:boundary}
Our tuned-chunk measurements stop at 4K inputs. To see what happens beyond, we fit a model of the scheduler step to the measured runs, check it against measurements it was not fitted to, and use it to map which side can meet both limits. Unlike Section~\ref{sec:h2hsec}, the map asks which side can meet both limits at low load, not how much load each serves per GPU. It is a prediction, not a measurement, and this analysis was not part of our pre-registered protocol.

\subsection{A step-time model}\label{sec:model}
A replica processes a prompt of $L$ tokens in chunks of $c$ tokens. A step that carries a chunk starting at prompt position $p$, together with the ongoing decodes, takes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:step}
t(c, p) = d + e + a\,c + b\,c\,(p + c/2),
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the decode share of the step, $e$ the fixed cost of a step that carries prefill, $a$ the per-token cost, and $b$ the cost of attending to the prefix and within the chunk. The longest gap of a request decoding alongside is the longest such step, which for long prompts is the last full chunk. At low load, without queueing, the replica's TTFT is the sum of its steps plus a fixed overhead $o$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ttftrep}
\mathrm{TTFT}_{\mathrm{rep}}(L, c) \approx o + \sum_k t(c_k, p_k) = o + n\,(d + e) + a\,L + b\,L^2/2,
\end{equation}
with $n = \lceil L/c \rceil$ steps. The chunk enters the TTFT only through the step count, but it enters the longest gap directly, and the longest gap grows with $L$ because later chunks attend to a longer prefix. A chunk small enough for the gap limit at long $L$ therefore needs many steps, and each step pays $d + e$. For 1P1D we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:ttft} with the prefill node running 8,192-token chunks: the same $aL + bL^2/2$ plus the transfer $V(L)/(m \times C_{\mathrm{flow}})$ and a 28~ms residual. We use $m = 1.5$, the low-load rate we measured for this checkpoint and the value checked in Table~\ref{tab:model}; Figure~\ref{fig:boundary} also shows $m = 1$, the rate at the loads that decided our verdicts, and $m = 1.8$, the low-load rate of transfers split four ways, as 26B transfers are above about 16K tokens. On the 1P1D side the gap limit applies to the decode node, whose longest gap grew with input length while its median inter-token latency did not (p90 14.4~ms at 4K and 27.8~ms at 16K; median 7.8 and 8.0~ms). We model it as the line through these two points; on g7 the same construction for 26B NVFP4 predicted the 32K value (43.3~ms, measured 43.3~ms). We did not identify the cause.

We fitted $e$, $a$, and $b$ by least squares to the longest gaps of 26B FP8 on g7e (the 90th percentile of the per-request longest gap, which at these rates is a step carrying a full chunk): chunks of 2,560, 1,024, and 512 tokens at 4K inputs, and 2,560 tokens at 16K inputs. We set $d$ to the measured median inter-token latency of those runs (7.0~ms) and $o$ from one TTFT (4K, 2,560 tokens). The fit gives $e = 9.0$~ms, $a = 15.1$~\textmu s per token, and $b = 2.65$~ns per token$^2$. We use 26B FP8 because it is the only model for which we measured 16K inputs on g7e; its BF16 KV cache is twice that of NVFP4 ($b_{\mathrm{g}} = 20$ and $b_{\mathrm{w}} = 200$~KiB per token in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vol}). Table~\ref{tab:model} compares the model with six measurements on g7e that it was not fitted to (replica TTFTs at three points within 8\%, 1P1D TTFTs at three points within 7\%) and with four on g7.

Figure~\ref{fig:boundary} plots, for each input length, the lowest TTFT limit each side can meet together with the gap limit. Above the blue curve, replicas meet both limits with the largest chunk whose longest step fits the gap limit (the largest admissible chunk); above the orange curve, 1P1D over TCP does. We call the shortest input beyond which the orange curve stays below the blue one the crossover length.
\begin{itemize}
  \item At 4K the model is close to our measurements: the largest admissible chunk for a 50~ms gap is about 1,600 tokens (TTFT 0.17~s; measured 1,024 tokens, 0.19~s) and for 30~ms about 560 (0.25~s; measured 512, 0.24~s); 1P1D needs 0.33~s at $m = 1.5$ (measured 0.36~s).
  \item At a 50~ms gap limit the crossover length is about 14K tokens, where both sides need about 0.86~s. Beyond it the admissible chunk shrinks (336 tokens at 32K) and 1P1D over TCP meets TTFT limits that replicas cannot (1.33 against 1.58~s at 20K, 2.5 against 3.5~s at 32K), until about 37K, where the decode node's own longest gap reaches 50~ms.
  \item At 30~ms the crossover length is about 6K (1.01 against 1.75~s at 16K), and 1P1D's advantage ends near 18K; beyond about 36K no chunk of 128 tokens or more fits.
  \item Under our strict SLO (1~s, 50~ms) the model predicts essentially no such region: replicas meet 1~s up to about 15.5K tokens and 1P1D up to about 16K. For a 3~s limit, the longest input is about 30K for replicas and 36K for 1P1D.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What moves the crossover}\label{sec:moves}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{KV size.} With the transfer volume of a KV cache whose sliding-window layers kept every token (220~KiB per token; 6.9~GiB at 32K against 840~MiB) and step times unchanged, 1P1D meets no TTFT limit that replicas miss at either gap limit before the decode node's limit (dash-dot); with global-layer shapes in every layer (120~KiB per token) the 50~ms crossover moves to about 48K, past the decode node's limit. Global attention would also lengthen the replicas' steps, which we did not model. A small KV cache relative to the link, here from hybrid attention, is what lets disaggregation over TCP take over.
  \item \textbf{Link speed.} With a transfer at 95~Gbps, ten times one TCP flow, the crossover moves to about 5.4K tokens at 50~ms and 2.8K at 30~ms (dotted), at TTFT limits of about 0.2~s; we kept the decode node's measured gap, which a faster link might change.
  \item \textbf{Gap limit.} A tighter limit moves the crossover to shorter inputs (about 6K at 30~ms against 14K at 50~ms), but the decode node's gap ends 1P1D's advantage sooner (18K against 37K).
  \item \textbf{Model error and transfer rate.} The fit has four points for three parameters, and the crossover depends mostly on the per-step cost $d + e$ (16~ms). At 50~ms and $m = 1.5$, refitting without each point in turn moves the crossover from 11K to 23K tokens; a joint fit to all nine gap points we measured for this checkpoint gives 14K (95\% range 11K--16K); correcting for the out-of-sample errors at 16K gives 11K; scaling $b$ by 0.9, which removes most of the 32K gap overestimate on g7, gives 15K. With $m = 1$ every value moves later, up to 34K. TTFT differences between chunks imply a per-step cost of 18--19~ms above 1,024 tokens but 11--12~ms below, consistent with CUDA graphs for steps of up to 512 tokens; this leaves the 50~ms crossover unchanged but moves the 30~ms one to about 10K. The model therefore places the crossover between roughly 11K and 34K tokens at 50~ms and 4K and 17K at 30~ms, not at a point.
  \item \textbf{Load.} At equal GPU count the 1P1D prefill node does two GPUs' prefill: at a 50~ms gap limit its prefill capacity per GPU is below the replicas' at every length (0.79 against 0.95 requests/s per GPU at 16K, 0.25 against 0.29 at 32K); at 30~ms it is higher, but the decode node's gap grows with load (at 16K, 48\% of requests exceeded 30~ms at 0.25 requests/s per GPU, against 7\% at 0.125). Our only 1P1D measurement beyond 16K (g7, 32K, 0.125 requests/s per GPU, before transfer tuning) had a median TTFT of 9.3~s against 3.5~s for replicas, with its prefill node about 85\% busy by our estimate. At long inputs, low load means rates below those we measured; we modeled no queueing.
  \item \textbf{Model and GPU.} A lighter model or a faster GPU (smaller $a$ and $b$) moves the crossover to longer inputs; at 4K, the median longest gap at a 2,560-token chunk was 48~ms for 26B NVFP4 against 62~ms for FP8.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Answer to RQ3.} Input length, through the step count. A gap limit caps the chunk, the cap shrinks as the prefix grows, and each extra step costs 11--19~ms. For 26B FP8 on g7e at low load, the model predicts that 1P1D over TCP meets TTFT limits that no admissible chunk meets from about 14K tokens at a 50~ms gap limit (11K--34K within its uncertainty) and about 6K at 30~ms (4K--17K), until the decode node's own longest gap reaches the limit, near 37K and 18K. The advantage is modest at 50~ms (2.5 against 3.5~s at 32K), needs TTFT limits of about a second or more, and concerns latency at low load, not goodput per GPU. It needs a small KV cache: with the transfer volume of a cache whose sliding-window layers kept every token, it disappears. These are predictions calibrated at 4K and 16K, not measurements.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A procedure for small-GPU fleets over TCP}\label{sec:procedure-fleet}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Measure the replicas' longest gap per request, not system percentiles, at the load you plan to serve.} With long inputs it approaches the time of a step carrying one full chunk; with chat it follows the longest co-scheduled prompt. It grows with load: at a 1,024-token chunk the median longest gap grew from 28~ms at 1 request/s per GPU to 42--43~ms at 5.
  \item \textbf{Use the largest chunk whose longest step fits the gap limit}, not the smallest the stack allows, and check that the stack allows it (Gemma~4 in vLLM v0.30.0 needs every modality disabled). In our 26B runs, 1,024 tokens added about 30~ms of median TTFT and passed 5 requests/s per GPU; 512 tokens added 70--80~ms at 1--2 and reached 359 against 200~ms at 4.
  \item \textbf{Check the TTFT that chunk gives} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ttftrep} or a measurement. If it meets the TTFT limit, stop: in our measurements, disaggregation over TCP did not serve more in that case.
  \item \textbf{Reduce prefill work before moving it.} If requests share prefixes, prefix-aware routing removes prefill without moving any KV cache. If KV caches are reused across nodes, loading a full prompt's cache moves at least $V(L)$ (only the matched prefix on a partial hit) if the store keeps the hybrid layout, which we did not check, and runs at $m \le 1$ over one connection. For 26B NVFP4 at 4K, NIXL's split pull moved 140~MiB in about 84~ms, against a colocated TTFT of about 130~ms of which prefill is only part; longer prompts favor loading.
  \item \textbf{Disaggregate over TCP only where no admissible chunk meets both limits} (in our model, 26B FP8 on g7e at a 50~ms gap: inputs beyond roughly 14K tokens, TTFT limits of a second or more, low load) \textbf{and the transfer fits}: estimate the TTFT with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ttft} using $V(L)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:vol}, the per-flow cap from one iperf flow, and $m$ measured at the target load (0.57--1.14 in our runs), and compare the offered KV traffic, request rate $\times\,V(L)$, with the rate transfers reach at that load (our 26B pair queued at 11.7~Gbps offered).
  \item \textbf{Fix the serving path}: raise UCX's TCP segment size (send and receive together, without pinning socket buffers), keep nodes in a placement group, use proxies that neither cap connections nor pause for garbage collection, and choose the prefill-to-decode ratio for the traffic (at 1:1, 31B chat kept the prefill GPU's streaming multiprocessors active only about 30\% of the time).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{RDMA, EFA, and scope}
EFA is available on the 8xlarge sizes of the same instance families (25~Gbps on g6e, 100~Gbps on g7 and g7e), with GPUDirect RDMA on g7.8xlarge but not on g6e.8xlarge or g7e.8xlarge~\cite{awsefa,awsspecs}, at 1.6--2$\times$ the hourly price (g7e \$5.27 against \$3.36, g7 \$4.09 against \$2.52, g6e \$4.53 against \$2.24)~\cite{awspricing}. Those sizes also have four times the vCPUs, and NIXL would use EFA through libfabric rather than UCX over TCP, so the comparison would change more than the network. EFA would shrink the transfer term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ttft}, but not the residual, the fixed split between the phases, or the replicas' chunk trade-off; in our model, a transfer ten times faster than one TCP flow moves the crossover to about 3--5.5K tokens. We did not measure EFA. The per-flow cap is an AWS policy, and NIXL's splitting rule belongs to one version; on other clouds and versions, measure one flow and several flows with iperf first.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item One model family (Gemma~4) with hybrid attention; models with only global attention have larger KV caches.
  \item One vLLM version (v0.30.0), one AWS availability zone, at most three nodes, and no RDMA or EFA baseline.
  \item Section~\ref{sec:boundary} is a model, calibrated on one model and GPU (26B FP8 on g7e) at 4K and 16K inputs and checked against six measurements on g7e and four on g7 that it was not fitted to; beyond 16K it extrapolates, it assumes low load, it omits queueing, and it locates the crossover only to within a factor of about two (roughly 11K--34K tokens at 50~ms). We did not measure tuned chunks on inputs longer than 4K.
  \item We measured chunks down to 512 tokens for 26B and 256 for 31B.
  \item Decisive points have one to five runs, and rates are resolved to 1 request/s per GPU; several verdicts are bounded by our highest measured rate.
  \item The disaggregated side kept most defaults (decode node limited to 256 sequences, NIXL's four threads; the prefill chunk was varied once).
  \item All but the last round used a replica proxy capped at 100 connections; we excluded the 11 runs that reached the cap (two of them also client-limited), but results from earlier rounds still used 2,560-token replicas.
  \item No prompt reuse; prefix-cache hits would shorten prefills and transfers. Latency was measured at the client and includes the proxy.
  \item Tuned-chunk replicas ran with every modality disabled, while the 2,560-token controls and the disaggregated servers kept only images disabled; we did not measure the effect on step time or KV capacity. Some comparisons join rounds from different days, and early rounds used a smaller client instance.
  \item Long-input requests used random tokens and a fixed 512-token output; real text may route 26B's experts differently.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
For a fleet of single-GPU instances linked by TCP, tune the chunk first. At the input lengths of most chat and short-document services, a chunk that keeps the longest gap within the limit costs tens of milliseconds of TTFT, and disaggregation over TCP served no more under any SLO we could rank; it is worth considering when the chunk cannot shrink, as with video input on Gemma~4 in vLLM v0.30.0. For long inputs, a gap limit forces chunks so small that the step count dominates TTFT, and our calibrated model predicts that at low load disaggregation over TCP then meets TTFT limits of a second or more that chunked prefill cannot, from roughly 14K tokens at a 50~ms gap limit (11K--34K within the model's uncertainty) until the decode node's own gap reaches the limit, near 37K, provided the KV cache is small, as with hybrid attention. The transfer itself is mostly a matter of software defaults and cloud policy: raise UCX's segment size, keep nodes in a placement group, and plan on about one flow's rate under load (long transfers reached 1.5--1.8 flows at low load). Measuring the long-input regime directly is the next step.

\section{Practical pitfalls}\label{app:pitfalls}
\subsection{Proxies that distort the measurement}\label{sec:proxy}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{v1_proxy_gc.pdf}
\caption{Global stalls in two 1P1D runs with the same seed (session E8; 26B, 4K input, 4 requests/s per GPU). Orange: number of requests whose output stream paused for more than 50~ms within the same 5~ms. Blue dashed: second-generation garbage collections in the proxy. (a) vLLM's test proxy. (b) Our aiohttp proxy.}
\label{fig:proxy}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Garbage collection in the disaggregated proxy.} Near their limits, disaggregated runs showed global pauses, which we saw in none of the five uncapped 1,024-token replica runs at 4--5 requests/s per GPU: up to five times per run at 4 requests/s per GPU, 16--113 in-flight requests (typically 35--110) paused together, for a median of 50--80~ms. In E8 we instrumented both proxies to log upstream stream gaps, event-loop lag, and garbage collection, and snapshotted the decode server's inter-token latency histogram before and after each point. For 26B 1P1D with vLLM's test proxy, four of five such events in three runs coincided within 100~ms with a second-generation garbage collection of 25--40~ms that blocked the proxy's event loop, after which the paused streams received a burst of tokens (Figure~\ref{fig:proxy}a). With our aiohttp proxy, second-generation collections took 9~ms and the loop never lagged. One event remained in three runs; the proxy received it from upstream, the decode server logged it as 113 gaps over 50~ms, and it appears at the same time in the test-proxy run with the same seed, so it is most likely a server-side pause. For 31B chat, the events with our aiohttp proxy were also server-side: the decode server logged 110 and 316 gaps over 50~ms, and the proxy showed no collection or lag. We instrumented only session E8, so we do not attribute earlier disaggregated failures, such as the three-node 2P1D collapse, to the proxy.

\paragraph{A connection limit in the replicas' proxy.} The replicas' round-robin proxy used an HTTP client whose default limit is 100 concurrent connections. At 4--6 requests/s per GPU in E8, every TTFT failure of 1,024-token replicas came from a request that arrived while 100 or more requests were in flight (33 of 33 at 4, 393 of 393 at 5), and no request failed on gaps at 5. With the limit removed in E9, the same configuration reached 1.000 at 4 and 0.999--1.000 at 5. The cap had lowered the replicas' supported load by one step and inflated their TTFT tail. It also shortened the gaps, because fewer requests decoded at once: at 5 requests/s per GPU the median inter-token latency was 17.3~ms with the cap and 30.3--31.8~ms without it. We therefore exclude capped runs even where they passed.

\subsection{Other pitfalls}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gemma~4 in vLLM v0.30.0 rejected chunks below 2,496 tokens with images, or images and audio, disabled, and accepted them with images, audio, and video all disabled; with all three disabled, a 2,560-token chunk fails at engine start.
  \item aiohttp's client limits a session to 100 concurrent connections by default; a proxy built on it queues requests silently beyond that.
  \item vLLM's test proxy for NIXL pauses all streams during garbage collection (Appendix~\ref{sec:proxy}); our aiohttp proxy had one keep-alive race in 1,440 requests.
  \item If UCX's TCP receive segment is smaller than the send segment, the engine refuses to start; raise both. In our runs, setting \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_SNDBUF} and \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_RCVBUF} to 8~MB left the receiver with a TCP window scale of 2, which caps the window at 256~KiB; check the kernel's \texttt{net.core.rmem\_max} and \texttt{wmem\_max} first, or leave them unset.
  \item At high rates the proxy hit the operating system's file-handle limit (1,024 by default); a disaggregated proxy opens two connections per request and hits it sooner.
  \item Trace replay in vLLM v0.30.0 without an explicit request count builds an empty request list.
  \item Replaying the same seeded requests against a server with prefix caching enabled makes later points hit the cache.
  \item New cloud workspaces create separate security groups; the client needs an explicit ingress rule to reach new nodes. Cluster placement groups may take 15--45 minutes to find capacity for a second node.
\end{itemize}

\section{Transfer settings}\label{app:ucx}
The tuned set used in sessions E4--E9, except the three E9 runs with an 8,192-token prefill chunk, which set only the two segment sizes:
{\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_TX\_SEG\_SIZE=256k}, \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_RX\_SEG\_SIZE=256k}
  \item \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_MAX\_IOV=16}, \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_MAX\_POLL=-1}
  \item \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_SNDBUF=8m}, \texttt{UCX\_TCP\_RCVBUF=8m}
  \item \texttt{UCX\_RNDV\_FRAG\_SIZE=host:4M,cuda:8M}
  \item with \texttt{UCX\_TLS=tcp,cuda\_copy,cuda\_ipc} and \texttt{UCX\_NET\_DEVICES=all} in every disaggregated run
\end{itemize}}
Session E8 showed that the two segment sizes alone give a slightly higher rate at low load (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The set requested 8~MB socket buffers; we did not change or record the kernel's socket limits, and the receiver's TCP window scale was 2 in both seven-variable runs we sampled, against 7 with the segment sizes alone or the defaults.

\paragraph{NIXL's split.} NIXL 1.4.1 splits a transfer's descriptor list into pieces of $\max(N/4, 1{,}024)$ descriptors, the last one smaller, so a list of fewer than 1,024 descriptors goes out as one piece. A 26B 4K transfer has about 1,900--2,240 descriptors at low load and goes out in two or three pieces, a 31B 4K transfer (about 4,000--4,500) in four, and transfers under 1,024 descriptors (26B inputs under about 590 tokens, 31B under about 290) as a single piece on a shared connection.

Other settings did not help. Staging the KV cache through host memory slowed TTFT 4.7$\times$ at 0.125 requests/s per GPU on g7 and had no stable load at 0.5. Push transfer (Figure~\ref{fig:push}), in which the prefill node writes the KV cache, cut 31B's transfer time by 27--36\% and median TTFT by 53--62~ms but changed attainment by at most 0.04; its logged volume was 468--533~MiB per request against 560 for pull, which we could not explain. Within one node over PCIe, push has been reported about 100$\times$ faster than pull~\cite{vllm52607}; we saw no such gap across nodes. A proposed vLLM patch that overlaps the transfer with prefill layer by layer\footnote{\url{https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/56874}} supports only models with a single KV-cache group; with hybrid-attention Gemma~4 it stalled on the first request. Network credits were not a constraint: g7e.2xlarge has a fixed 50~Gbps, and on g6e.2xlarge (5~Gbps baseline, 20~Gbps burst) our 15-minute test load averaged about 1.2~Gbps, computed from the request rate and volume, below the baseline.


\section[Deviations from the pre-registered protocol]{Deviations from the pre-registered protocol\protect\footnote{Protocol v1.7, committed on 24 September 2026 before the first measurement round (commit \texttt{1296b54} in the public repository); not externally timestamped.}}\label{app:dev}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gap limit: five times each model's decode step became a common 50~ms (Section~\ref{sec:slo}). One-token outputs pass the gap criterion since session E8.
  \item Chunk size: 2,048 was planned; Gemma~4 forced 2,560 until sessions E8 and E9 swept 256--2,048.
  \item Proxy: one proxy for both approaches was planned; replicas and disaggregated runs used different proxies, and the replicas' proxy was capped at 100 connections until E9.
  \item Equivalence band: 0.9--1.1 was planned; Figure~\ref{fig:who} uses 0.8--1.25.
  \item Confidence intervals: a request-level bootstrap was planned; we report hierarchical bootstrap intervals for decisive points (Appendix~\ref{app:ci}).
  \item Client: a fixed c6i.2xlarge was planned; sessions before E7 used c6i.xlarge. Warm-up was implemented from E7 on.
  \item Reference points on H100 and on a two-GPU g5 node were not measured.
  \item The step-time model of Section~\ref{sec:boundary} is a post-hoc analysis that the protocol did not include.
  \item UCX: the three E9 runs with an 8,192-token prefill chunk set only the two segment sizes, which the E9 predictions stated.
\end{itemize}
